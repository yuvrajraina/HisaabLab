Choosing a home loan tenure is not simply a choice between a “low EMI” and a “high EMI.” The tenure changes how long principal remains outstanding, how much interest can accumulate and how much room remains in the household budget each month.

A useful comparison therefore needs at least three numbers: **monthly EMI, total interest and total repayment**. It should also test whether the EMI remains manageable after ordinary commitments and an unexpected change in income or interest rate.

This guide compares four tenures using one consistent example. The figures are calculated with HisaabLab’s reducing-balance EMI model and rounded to the nearest rupee for readability.

## Quick comparison: ₹50 lakh at 8.5%

Assume an illustrative home loan with:

- principal of **₹50,00,000**;
- annual interest rate of **8.5%**;
- monthly payments at the end of each month;
- no fees, insurance, prepayments or rate changes; and
- the same rate for every tenure.

| Loan tenure | Approximate EMI | Total interest | Total repayment |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 15 years | ₹49,237 | ₹38,62,656 | ₹88,62,656 |
| 20 years | ₹43,391 | ₹54,13,879 | ₹1,04,13,879 |
| 25 years | ₹40,261 | ₹70,78,406 | ₹1,20,78,406 |
| 30 years | ₹38,446 | ₹88,40,443 | ₹1,38,40,443 |

These are educational estimates, not lender quotations. A lender’s result may differ because of the disbursal date, day-count convention, rate resets, broken-period interest, fees or rounding.

You can reproduce the comparison with different assumptions in the [Home Loan EMI Calculator](/calculators/home-loan-emi/).

## The central trade-off: EMI falls slowly while interest keeps rising

Moving from 15 to 20 years lowers the estimated EMI by about **₹5,846 per month**, but adds roughly **₹15.51 lakh** of total interest under the unchanged-rate example.

Moving from 20 to 25 years provides a smaller reduction of about **₹3,130 per month**, while adding approximately **₹16.65 lakh** of interest.

Extending from 25 to 30 years lowers the payment by only about **₹1,815 per month**, yet adds around **₹17.62 lakh** of interest.

This pattern occurs because each extension leaves principal outstanding for longer. The extra years can improve required monthly cash flow, but the reduction in EMI becomes progressively smaller in this example. Looking only at the EMI hides that cost.

## How the EMI formula creates this result

For principal **P**, monthly rate **r** and **n** monthly payments, the standard repayment formula is:

**EMI = P × r × (1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)**

Increasing **n** spreads repayment across more instalments. Each instalment can be smaller, but interest continues to be charged on the opening balance every month. Early payments generally contain more interest because the outstanding principal is then at its highest.

The detailed mechanics are explained in the [home loan EMI and amortisation guide](/blog/home-loan-emi-formula-amortisation-india/).

## When a 15-year tenure may fit

A 15-year schedule has the highest required EMI in the comparison and the lowest total interest. It may suit a household whose cash flow can support the payment without using money needed for essential expenses, emergency reserves, insurance or near-term goals.

The lower lifetime interest does not make the shortest possible tenure automatically correct. A payment that leaves no monthly buffer can create its own risk. The contractual EMI is due even during an expensive month, while a voluntary prepayment can usually be delayed when cash flow is tight—subject to the loan’s actual terms.

Before selecting the shorter tenure, test the EMI against take-home income and recurring commitments with the [Home Affordability Calculator](/calculators/home-affordability/).

## What changes with a 20-year tenure

In the example, the 20-year EMI is about 11.9% lower than the 15-year EMI. That difference can create monthly breathing room, but the loan remains outstanding for five additional years and estimated interest rises materially.

A 20-year term can be a middle path when the 15-year payment is uncomfortable but a very long schedule is unnecessary. The decision still depends on the household’s actual cash flow, income stability, other debt and purchase costs—not on the tenure label alone.

## What 25 and 30 years really buy

The longest tenures provide the lowest required payments in the table. Their main benefit is contractual cash-flow flexibility, not low total cost.

That distinction matters. Choosing 30 years instead of 20 years lowers the illustrative EMI by about **₹4,945 per month**, but total interest is roughly **₹34.27 lakh higher** if the rate and scheduled payments never change.

A longer tenure may be useful when preserving liquidity is important, but it should be evaluated with a plan rather than an assumption that future income will certainly rise. If the intention is to make extra payments later, model the amount and timing explicitly in the [Home Loan Prepayment Calculator](/calculators/home-loan-prepayment/). Also verify how the lender applies a prepayment and whether any loan-specific conditions or charges apply.

## A five-step way to choose a tenure

### 1. Set a household EMI ceiling

Start with take-home income, existing EMIs and recurring commitments. Leave room for expenses that do not appear in a loan application, including maintenance, insurance, education, healthcare and irregular annual bills.

A lender’s eligibility assessment answers a different question from personal affordability. HisaabLab’s affordability threshold is editable because no single percentage can represent every household.

### 2. Preserve cash outside the purchase

The down payment is not the only upfront requirement. Stamp duty, registration, brokerage, furnishing, repairs and moving costs may also require cash. Avoid treating every available rupee as part of the down payment without considering the post-purchase buffer.

Use the [True Cost of Home Ownership Calculator](/calculators/true-cost-of-homeownership/) to separate purchase cash, financing cost and recurring ownership expenses.

### 3. Compare interest, not only EMI

For every proposed tenure, write down:

- the required monthly EMI;
- total repayment;
- total interest;
- the balance remaining after important milestones; and
- the effect of one or more realistic prepayments.

The cheapest EMI is not necessarily the cheapest loan. A lower payment can simply mean more payments.

### 4. Stress-test the rate

The table assumes 8.5% forever. That is a modelling input, not a market forecast. A floating-rate loan can reset, changing EMI, tenure or both according to the contract and applicable framework.

The Reserve Bank of India’s [FAQ on reset of floating interest rates](https://old.rbi.org.in/commonman/english/scripts/FAQs.aspx?Id=3687) describes communication and borrower options for covered EMI-based personal loans. Use the [Fixed vs Floating Rate Calculator](/calculators/fixed-vs-floating-rate/) to test higher and lower hypothetical rate paths rather than relying on a single forecast.

### 5. Compare the lender’s complete disclosure

Before accepting a loan, reconcile the calculator with the lender’s current quotation and contractual documents. Compare the annual percentage rate or equivalent cost disclosure, processing and legal charges, reset terms, insurance, prepayment treatment and the amortisation schedule.

RBI’s [consumer guidance on home loans](https://systemhealth.rbi.org.in/Scripts/FS_FAQs.aspx?Id=77&fn=2754) provides general borrower education on comparing rates, fees and loan terms. The lender’s current Key Facts Statement, sanction letter and agreement remain decisive for the specific loan.

## Common tenure-comparison mistakes

**Comparing EMIs at different interest rates.** To isolate the effect of tenure, keep principal and rate identical first. Compare rate offers separately afterward.

**Assuming a 30-year loan will definitely be prepaid early.** Model an actual prepayment amount and schedule. A future intention is not the same as a completed payment.

**Ignoring the remaining balance.** After several years, two options may have very different outstanding principal even if their EMIs look similar.

**Using gross income but budgeting from take-home income.** Base the household stress test on money actually available after deductions and unavoidable commitments.

**Removing the emergency buffer to obtain a shorter tenure.** Lower interest is useful, but contractual payments must remain resilient.

**Treating an illustrative rate as current or guaranteed.** Enter the lender’s actual quote and test possible resets.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a 15-year home loan always better than a 20-year loan?

It has lower total interest when principal and rate are the same, but the higher required EMI may not suit every household. The better fit is the shortest contractual payment that remains comfortable after commitments and a realistic safety buffer.

### Is it better to choose 30 years and prepay?

That can provide a lower required EMI with optional extra payments, but the result depends on whether the prepayments actually happen, when they occur and how the lender applies them. Compare a defined prepayment plan with the shorter contractual schedule rather than assuming identical outcomes.

### Why does extending from 25 to 30 years reduce EMI so little?

At long tenures, the payment already contains a large interest component. Adding more months spreads principal further but also keeps interest running longer, so the incremental EMI relief can become small relative to the additional total interest.

### What happens if a floating rate increases?

Depending on the contract and lender response, the EMI may rise, the tenure may extend or both may change. Stress-test multiple paths and review the lender’s reset communication and options.

### Should tenure be chosen using current salary or expected future salary?

Use current, dependable cash flow for the contractual obligation. Possible future income growth can be tested as an upside scenario, but it should not be treated as certain.
