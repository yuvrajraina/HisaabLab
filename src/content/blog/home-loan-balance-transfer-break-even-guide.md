A lower advertised home-loan rate does not by itself prove that a balance transfer will save money. The decision starts with the current outstanding balance and remaining schedule, then compares the proposed loan after every switching cost and any change in tenure.

The useful question is not “How much lower is the rate?” It is “Will the future cost reduction exceed the upfront cost before I expect this loan to end or change again?”

## Information to collect before comparing

Use current documents rather than the original sanction figures:

- outstanding principal on a specific date;
- current annual rate and benchmark or reset terms;
- remaining number of EMIs;
- proposed annual rate and whether it is fixed or floating;
- proposed tenure;
- processing, legal, valuation, documentation and administrative costs;
- any conversion, closure or other applicable charge; and
- the date from which the new schedule would begin.

Ask both lenders for written disclosures. The Reserve Bank of India’s [home-loan consumer guidance](https://systemhealth.rbi.org.in/Scripts/FS_FAQs.aspx?Id=77&fn=2754) highlights APR, fees, reset clauses and switching costs as important comparison points. Current regulations, lender category and contract terms remain decisive.

## Compare the same outstanding balance

Both schedules should begin from the same principal on the same date. Comparing the proposed loan with the original loan amount mixes past and future cash flows.

Interest already paid is sunk. The transfer decision should compare:

1. remaining interest and payments if the current loan continues; and
2. future interest and payments under the proposed loan, plus switching costs.

The [Home Loan Balance Transfer Calculator](/calculators/home-loan-balance-transfer/) re-amortises both options from the same entered outstanding balance.

## Net savings and break-even

A simplified net-savings calculation is:

**Net savings = old future interest − new future interest − switching costs**

If the proposed EMI is lower, a simple monthly break-even estimate is:

**Break-even months = switching costs ÷ monthly payment reduction**

That estimate needs interpretation. A lower EMI caused by extending tenure can delay repayment and increase total interest even when the rate falls. Always compare the number of payments and total future interest, not only the monthly difference.

## Worked example

Consider an illustrative comparison:

- outstanding principal: ₹40,00,000;
- current rate: 9%;
- remaining tenure: 180 months;
- proposed rate: 8%;
- proposed tenure: 180 months; and
- switching costs: ₹50,000.

The model calculates both EMIs and future interest from ₹40 lakh. Net savings subtract the ₹50,000 cost. If the borrower changes the proposed tenure to 240 months, the EMI may fall further—but the additional payment months can materially change total interest.

The correct conclusion depends on the complete result and how long the borrower expects to keep the loan.

## Rate type and reset risk

Confirm whether the proposed rate is floating, fixed for the full tenure or fixed only for an initial period. A headline introductory rate followed by a reset is not equivalent to a full-tenure fixed rate.

For covered EMI-based personal loans, the RBI’s [FAQ on floating-rate resets](https://old.rbi.org.in/commonman/english/scripts/FAQs.aspx?Id=3687) discusses borrower communications and options when benchmark changes affect EMI or tenor. Read the proposed contract’s benchmark, spread, reset frequency and conversion terms.

Use the [Fixed vs Floating Rate Calculator](/calculators/fixed-vs-floating-rate/) to test rate paths separately. A balance-transfer calculator should not pretend to forecast future benchmarks.

## When break-even can be misleading

**You expect to prepay soon.** There may be too little time to recover switching costs.

**The new tenure is longer.** EMI savings can coexist with a higher total repayment.

**Important costs are missing.** Legal, valuation or documentation charges can change the result.

**The rate can reset quickly.** A temporary difference may not persist until break-even.

**The current lender offers an internal conversion.** A lower-cost rate conversion may deserve comparison with a full transfer.

## A practical transfer checklist

Before proceeding, confirm:

1. the exact outstanding amount and closure process;
2. every old-lender and new-lender charge;
3. the proposed benchmark, spread and reset frequency;
4. whether the tenure changes;
5. the net future-interest saving after costs;
6. the break-even date;
7. the document-release and security-transfer process; and
8. that the payment remains affordable under a higher-rate scenario.

Do not transfer solely because the new EMI is smaller. A good comparison preserves the same start date and balance, makes tenure visible and counts every known cost.

## Frequently asked questions

### How large must the rate difference be for a transfer?

There is no universal minimum. Outstanding balance, remaining tenure, costs and how long the new rate persists determine the result.

### Is a lower EMI proof that the transfer saves money?

No. The new loan may have more payment months. Compare future interest, fees and total repayment.

### Should I include taxes on fees?

Include every amount you expect to pay to complete the transfer. Use current written quotations rather than a generic percentage.

### What if my current lender offers a rate conversion?

Model it as another scenario. An internal conversion may have lower friction, but its rate, fee and terms still require comparison.
